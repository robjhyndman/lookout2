\section{Comparing new lookout with the older version (SK)} \label{sec:comparewitholderversion}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
Next we compare the old and the new versions of lookout. We conduct 4 experiments that explore different aspects of the updated algorithm and illustrate the output using a set of showcase examples. All experiments have anomalies and non-anomalies. Experiments 1 and 2 consider the anomalies moving away from the non-anomalous distribution and investigate the resulting performance. These two experiments compare how quickly the old and new versions of lookout identify anomalies as they become more obvious.  Experiments 3 and 4 focus on having anomalies on the boundary of the non-anomalous region. For these two experiments we increase the number of data points and explore the performance of the two algorithm versions.

% - Show anomaly detection examples using normal distribution and gamma distribution, with lookout 2 vs lookout 1

\subsection{Experiment 1:  Different anomaly rates for Gamma distribution}
For experiment 1 we consider the  $\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ distribution with shape $\alpha$ and rate $\beta$ and generate data points with different rates $\beta$.  We select $\text{Gamma}(2, 2)$ as the non-anomalous distribution and $\text{Gamma}(2, r)$  as the anomalous distribution where $r$ changes with each iteration of the experiment with $r \in \{0.1, 0.2, \ldots, 1\}$. We let $X_i \in \mathbb{R}^2$ with $X_i = (X_{i1}, X_{i2})$ where $X_{i1}, X_{i2} \sim \text{Gamma}(2, 2)$ for the non-anomalous distribution and $X_{i1}, X_{i2} \sim \text{Gamma}(2, r)$ for the anomalous distributions. We consider 500 non-anomalies and 10 anomalies in each iteration. Each iteration is repeated 10 times to account for randomness.

\cref{fig:exp1GammaRates}  shows the data and results of Experiment 1. As the rate $r$ of the anomalous Gamma distribution increases, the anomalies and non-anomalies become similar. Thus, we expect a decrease in the true positive rate and an increase in the false negative rate as $r$ increases. The anomalies are maximally different from non-anomalies when $r = 0.1$. We see that new lookout performs better for these cases compared to the old version. Old lookout oscillates between high and low performances for $r = 0.1$, whereas the new version consistently performs well.

\subsection{Experiment 2:  Different anomaly means for Normal distribution}

For experiment 2 we consider the normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma)$ with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$. As before we consider separate $\mu$ and $\sigma$ for non-anomalous and anomalous distributions. We let $X_i = (X_{i1}, X_{i2}) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ with $X_{i1}, X_{i2} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma)$. The non-anomalous distribution is  $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. The anomalous distribution has $\mu \in \{ 2.50, 2.75,  3.00 , 3.25,  3.50, 3.75, 4.00\} $ and $\sigma = 0.2$. We consider 1000 non-anomalies and 10 anomalies and repeat every iteration (for each $\mu$) 10 times.

\subsection[Experiment 3: As N increases - Normal distribution]{Experiment 3: As $N$ increases - Normal distribution}
With realworld datasets such as credit card transactions data, often it is difficult to identify fraudulent transactions (anomalies) from legitimate transactions because they are similar. Thus, what we consider to be anomalies are not well separated from the non-anomalous points. This experiment touches upon this aspect of anomaly detection.  We check the effectiveness of \textit{lookout} when anomalies are near the boundary of non-anomalous points.

We consider $n$ non-anomalies for $n \in \{1000, 2000, \ldots, 10000\}$ and $0.005 \times n$ anomalies. We consider $X_i = (X_{i1}, X_{i1}) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ with $X_{i1}, X_{i2} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma)$ with $\mu = 0$ and $\sigma = 1$ for non-anomalies. We generate anomalies $Y_i = (Y_{i1}, Y_{i2})$ on the boundary of the non-anomalous region by sampling $Y_{i1} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{i1}, 0.1)$ and  $Y_{i2} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{i2}, 0.1)$ where the means $\mu_{i1}$ and $\mu_{i2}$ are sampled from the boundary of a ball of radius $\sqrt{2} \times 2.2$. We do this by first sampling $\mu_{i1} \sim \mathcal{U}(-\sqrt{2}\times 2.2, \sqrt{2}\times 2.2)$ and letting $\mu_{i2} = \sqrt{2\times2.2^2 - \mu_{i1}^2}$. Then we sample the anomalies $Y_{i1} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{i1}, 0.1)$ and  $Y_{i2} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{i2}, 0.1)$.  Each iteration for a given $n$ is repeated 10 times to account for randomness.

\subsection[Experiment 4: As N increases - Gamma distributions]{Experiment 4: As $N$ increases - Gamma distribution}
This experiment is similar to experiment 3, but uses a Gamma distribution instead. As before, the aim is to check the effectiveness of \textit{lookout} when anomalies are near the boundary of non-anomalous points. For this experiment we consider $X_i \in \mathbb{R}^2$ with $X_i = (X_{i1}, X_{i2})$. We consider $n$ non-anomalies $X_i$ with  $X_{i1}, X_{i2} \sim \text{Gamma}(2, 2)$ where $n \in \{1000, 2000, \ldots, 10000\}$ in each iteration. The anomalies are generated somewhat differently. We first generate $n$ points $Y_i = (Y_{i1}, Y_{i2})$ from $\text{Gamma}(2.2, 2)$. From this set of points we sample a subset that are on the outer periphery of $X_i$. We do this by considering the subset $\{Y_i: \lVert Y_i \rVert >  \lVert X\rVert _{0.99} \}$ where $\lVert Y_i \rVert = \sqrt{ Y_{i1}^2 + Y_{i2}^2} $ denotes the distance from the origin to $Y_i$ and $\lVert X\rVert _{0.99}$ denotes the 99th percentile of $\left \{\sqrt{ X_{i1}^2 + X_{i2}^2} \right \}_{i=1}^n$. Of this subset we sample $0.005 \times n$ points and label them as anomalies. Each iteration for a given $n$ is repeated 10 times.  \cref{fig:exp4GammaIncreasingN} shows the data from an iteration of the experiment with $n = 10000$ and the results for the new and old version of \textit{lookout}. Again we see that new lookout performs better in terms of true positive and false negative rates.

\subsection{Showcase examples}\label{sec:realworld}

\cref{fig:showcaseExamples} shows some examples of new and old versions of lookout with identified anomalies in red. The purpose of this exercise is to check if the new version agrees with the old version on obvious anomalies. The graphs on the first row have anomalies at the top right hand corner. The second row depicts a bi-modal distribution with anomalies placed in the middle. Due to sparsity of points both versions of lookout identify some non-anomalous points as anomalies. The third row has 3 clusters of normally distributed points with anomalies placed around the point $(6, 6)$. The same setting is repeated in the fourth row with the anomalies spreaded out. The last row has non-anomalous points in a parabola and the anomalous points outside of it. This is a tricky example as the points are on a manifold and we are not considering their geometry. In this example the new version of lookout identifies 2 of the 3 anomalies, but also identifies the boundary points as anomalous. In contrast, the old version of lookout does not identify any of the 3 anomalies, but identifies 2 boundary points as anomalous. Apart from the last example, the overall performance of new and old versions of lookout is similar on these obvious cases.

\subsection{Real-world Examples}

We check the new and old versions of lookout on two real world examples. Neither of these examples have labeled anomalies and as such we cannot compute accuracy measures. However, as both datasets have a small number of variables, we can visualize the output. The datasets are
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Old faithful dataset \citep{weirdBook}: this dataset contains eruptions of the the Old Faithful Geyser in Yellowstone National Park, Wyoming, USA, from 1 January 2015 to 1 October 2021. For each recorded eruption the duration is given in seconds as well as the waiting time to the following eruption. The records are incomplete, especially during winter.
	\item Wine reviews dataset \citep{weirdBook}: this dataset contains different wine attributes from 44 countries taken from Wine Enthusiast Magazine during the week of 15 June 2017. We focus on the numerical attributes in this data: points given by Wine Enthusiast reviewer on a scale of 0-100 and the price of a bottle of wine.
\end{enumerate}
