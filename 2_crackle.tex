\section{Consistency and crackle}\label{sec:theory}

We will first prove a number of limit theorems about $d_{\omega(n)}$, that is the $\omega(n)$-th death time (when ordered increasingly by size). For consistency of the kernel density estimator, we want to show $d_{\omega(n)} \to 0$ and $n(d_{\omega(n)})^{m/2}\to \infty$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:nd_f(n) in plane}
  Let $d_k$ denote the $k$th smallest death time of the $0$-homology for the Rips filtrations of a set of $n$ points sampled from probability distribution $X$ over $\R^m$ such that the probability density function exists and is bounded above. Note that there are $n-1$ finite death times. Choose any sequence of integers $\{\omega_n\}$ such that $0<\omega_n <n$ for all $n$ and $\lim_{n\to \infty} \omega_n =\infty$. Then $n(d_{\omega_n})^{m/2}\to \infty$ as $n\to \infty$ almost surely.
\end{theorem}

Each death time corresponds to the reduction by one of the number of connected components. This implies that $d_{\omega_n} > r_n$ occurs when $C(n, r_n) > n - \omega_n$.

  Now $C(n, r_n)$ is a random variable with range contained in $[1,n]$. This implies we can the upper bound the expectation by

\subsection{Limit of $d_{n-1}$ for Gaussian Samples}

In this section we will prove the complementary limit theorem needed for $d_{\omega(n)}$ to be an admissible bandwidth when we assume that the samples come from a Gaussian distribution. Since $d_{\omega(n)}\leq d_{n-1}$ it is sufficient to show $d_{n-1} \to \infty$ as $n\to \infty$. The proof is a straightforward modification of the proof for Theorem 2.1 in \cite{Crackle}. The only real change is that we need to consider a sequence of decreasing radii for the balls, whereas in \cite{Crackle} the radii remain constant (value $1$). We just need to ensure that the radii decrease slowly enough so that the union of balls remains connected. Due to this variation in the proof, we include the calculations for completeness.
%For consistency in the case of
% Gaussian distributions over $\R^2$, we also need to show that the
% index function for the maximum gap in death values will go to
% infinity almost surely.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:d_n-1 Gaussian}
  Let $S_n$ denote a random sample of $n$ iid points from a fixed multivariate Gaussian in $\R^m$. Then for the Rips filtration over $S_n$ we have $d_{n-1} \to 0$ as $n\to \infty$ almost surely.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  We will first prove the result in the case of a standard Gaussian, where the probability density function is spherically symmetric and is $c \exp\{-\|x\|^2/2\}$ for some constant $c$. Denote $f(r) = c\exp\{-r^2/2\}$ for this density in terms of the radial coordinate.

  %  Set $r(n)$ to satisfy $r(n)^d c \log \log n = 1$. By construction $r(n)\rightarrow 0$ as $n \rightarrow \infty$. We will use $r(n)$ to show that the core is covered by balls around the samples with radius $r(n)$.
  Set $R_n:=\sqrt{2(\log n+m\log \log n)}$ and  $C_n=\sqrt{2(\log n -\log \log n)}$.
  $R_n$ will be the radius outside which we will expect no samples to occur and $C_n$ will represent the radius of the core of the point cloud - which we expect to be densely covered.

  Cover $B(0, C_n)$ by a grid with side lengths $g_n$. Let $Y(n, C_n, g_n)$ be the event that at least one of the boxes in this grid is empty. Equation 3.1 in \cite{Crackle} states:
  $$
  \mathbb{P}(Y(n,C_n,g_n)) \leq (2g_n^{-1})m (C_n)^m\exp\{-ng_n^m f(C_n)\}.
  $$
  %Set $R_n^c=\sqrt{2(\log n -\log \log n)}$, which will represent the core of the point cloud.
  Set $g_n$
  %=\frac{r(n)}{2\sqrt{d}}$
  so that $g_n^m=\frac{1}{c \log \log n}$. 
  Further note that $f(C_n)= c \exp\{-\log n+\log\log n\}=c\frac{\log n}{n}$. 
  Combined we get
  \begin{align*}
    \mathbb{P}(Y(n,C_n,g_n))
    & \leq 2^{m}c \log \log n (2(\log n -\log \log n))^{m/2}\exp\left(\frac{-nc\log n}{cn\log \log n}\right)
    \leq k\frac{(\log n)^{m/2}\log\log n }{\exp(\frac{\log n}{\log \log n})}
  \end{align*}
  where $k$ is a constant independent of $n$. For large $n$, $\log n>(m\log\log n) \log \log n$ and thus
  $$
  \exp\left(\frac{\log n}{\log \log n}\right)>\exp(m\log\log n)=(\log n)^m.
  $$
  Thus for large $n$ we can bound
  $$
  \mathbb{P}\left(Y\left(n,C_n, g_n\right)\right)\leq k \frac{\log\log n }{(\log n)^{m/2}}
  $$
  which goes to $0$ as $n \to \infty$.

  %Set $R_n^2:=\sqrt{2(\log n+d\log \log n)}$.

  Let $X(n, R_n)$ be the event that all the samples lie inside $B(0, R_n)$.
  From Theorem 4 in \cite{Crackle} we have $\lim_{n\to \infty} \E[|S_n \cap B(0,R_n)^c|]=0$. As $|S_n \cap B(0,R_n)^c|$ must be an integer between $0$ and $n$ this implies that $\lim_{n\to \infty} \mathbb{P}(X(n,R_n))=1$.

  %that the probability of there being a point outside the ball centered at the origin with radius $R_n:=\sqrt{2(\log n +d\log\log n)}$ goes to zero as $n\to \infty$. This implies that we can assume all points are within $B(0,R_n)$ and, from our grid size, that every point in $B(0, C_n)$ is within $\frac{2\sqrt{d}}{ c \log \log n}$ of a sample point.

  We want to bound the distance from any point in $B(0, R_n)$ to its closest point in $B(0, C_n)$ which is the same as bounding $R_n-C_n$.
  We have $R_n+C_n= \sqrt{2(\log n +m\log\log n)}+\sqrt{2(\log n -\log \log n)} > 2\sqrt{\log n}$ so
  \begin{align*}
    R_n-C_n &=\frac{R_n^2-C_n^2}{R_n+C_n}\\
    &<\frac{2(\log n +m\log\log n)-2(\log n -\log \log n)}{2\sqrt{\log n}}\\
    &=\frac{(m+1)\log\log n}{\sqrt{\log n}}
  \end{align*}

  Suppose that both $Y(n, C_n, g_n)$ does not hold (every grid box in the covering the core contains at least one sample) and $X(n, R_n)$ does hold (all samples within a ball of radius $R_n$). That is every sample lies in $B(0,R_n)$ and there is a sample in every box in the grid over $C_n$. Set
  $$
  r(n):= \frac{2\sqrt{m}}{(c\log \log n)^{1/m}} + \frac{(m+1)\log\log n}{\sqrt{\log n}}
  $$
  which was chosen so $r(n)>(R_n-C_n) + 2\sqrt{m}g_n$.
  By construction we have $\cup_{x\in S_n}B(x, r(n))$ must be connected. This is because every sample is within $(R_n-C_n)$ of a grid point and every grid point is within $2\sqrt{m}g_n$ of a sample point.
  
  This implies that $d_{n-1}\leq r(n)$ as $d_{n-1}$ is the supremum of radii $r$ where $\cup_{x\in S_n}B(x, r)$ is disconnected.

  %This then implies that every point within $R_n^c$
  %Set $r(n)$ to satisfy $r(n)^d c \log \log n = 1$. By construction $r(n)\rightarrow 0$ as $n \rightarrow \infty$.

  %  Let $s(n)=r(n) + (d+1)\frac{\log\log n}{\sqrt{\log n}}$.
  %  Let $X(n,r(n))$ be the event that $\cup_{x\in S_n} B(x, s(n))$ is connected.
  As both $Y(n, C_n, g_n)$ and $X(n, R_n)$ occur with probability $1$ as $n$ grows to infinity we have $d_{n-1}\leq r(n) \to 0$ as $n\to \infty$ almost surely.

  %  We want to show that %there is a function $r(n)$ such that $r(n)\rightarrow 0$ as $n \rightarrow \infty$ and
  %  $\mathbb{P}(X(n,s(n)))\rightarrow 1$ as $n\rightarrow \infty$.
  %  This implies that $d_{n-1}\leq s(n)$ must also limit to $0$.

  %  %Let $s(n)=r(n) + (d+1)\frac{\log\log n}{\sqrt{\log n}}$.
  %  Then by the above arguments $\P(X(n,s(n)))\rightarrow 1$ as $n\rightarrow \infty$. This implies that $0<d_{n-1}<s(n)$. We also have $s(n)\to 0$ as $n\to \infty$. Together, they squeeze $d_{n-1}$ to satisfy $d_{n-1}\to 0$ as $n\to \infty$.

  To then extend the result to general multivariate Gaussian distributions, observe that our sample of points $\hat{S}_n$ is the linear transformation $T$ of a sample of points $S_n$ of the standard Gaussian. If $\cup_{x\in S_n} B(x, r)$ is connected, then we can infer that $\cup_{x\in S_n} B(T(x), \|T\|r)$ is also connected. This implies that $d_{n-1}(\hat{S}_n)\leq \|T\|d_{n-1}(S_n)$ and thus $d_{n-1}(\hat{S}_n) \to 0$ as $n\to \infty$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:d_*}
  For point clouds sampled from multivariate Gaussian distributions, we have $d_* \to 0$ as $n\to \infty$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
  Note that $i_*< n-1$ and so $d_*=d_{i_*}\leq d_{n-1}$ which we know limits to $0$ almost surely from \cref{thm:d_n-1 Gaussian}.
\end{proof}

We would like to combine the previous results to show that $\bm{H} = d_*\bm{I}_m$ results in a consistent density estimator of Gaussian samples in $\R^m$.  
The only remaining task is to show $i(n) \to \infty$ as $n \to \infty$ almost surely. This seems intuitively clear, but technically challenging. It is not the focus of the new version of lookout, and thus we will leave this proof as an open problem.

\subsection{A counterexample of a $d_{\omega(n)}$ which is not an admissible bandwidth}
%Consequences of crackle for distributions with fat tails}

There are many potential indexing functions $\omega(n)$ that we can choose to decide which death time to use to compute the bandwidth matrix. We wish for this choice $d_{\omega(n)}$ to be an admissible bandwidth we need $d_{\omega(n)} \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$.

If our samples are Gaussian, then we know that $d_{\omega(n)}$ leads is an admissible bandwidth whenever $\lim_{n\to \infty} \omega(n)=\infty$. However, when samples come from a fat-tailed distribution, we can construct examples where with positive probability $d_{\omega(n)}$ does not converge to $0$, which implies that $d_{\omega(n)}$ is not an admissible bandwidth.

Consider the distribution on $\R^m$ with the density function $f(x)=\frac{c_m}{1+\|x\|^{m+1}}$ where $c_m$ is the appropriate constant. For each radius $R$ the probability a sample point has modulus at least $R$ (that is, it lies outside $B(0, R)$) is
$$
p_{R}=\int_{R}^\infty \frac{c_m s_{m-1}r^{m-1}}{1+r^{m+1}}\,dr
$$
%For $R_n=\frac{n^{2/d}}{2}$ then
where $s_{m-1}$ is the volume of the $(m-1)$-sphere.
We have $p_{R}\in (\frac{c_m s_{m-1}}{2R}, \frac{c_m s_{m-1}}{R})$ for large enough $R$.

%number of samples with modulus at least $R$ is a binomial distribution with probability $$p_R=\int_{R}^\infty \frac{c_m s_{m-1}r^m-1}}{1+r^{m+1}}\,dr$$
%where $s_{m-1}$ is the area of the $(d-1)$-sphere. We have $p_R\in (\frac{c_m s_{m-1}}{2R}, \frac{c_m s_{m-1}}{R})$ for large enough $R$.

Set $R_n=\frac{2n^{2/m}}{c_m s_{m-1}}$. Let $S_n$ be the number of samples that lie outside $B(0, R_n)$.
We have $S_n \sim \mathcal{B}(n, p_{R_n})$, the binomial distribution with $p_{[R_n, \infty)}\in (n^{-2/m}, 2n^{-2/m})$. This implies that there is a $\delta_1>0$ independent of $n$ such that
$\mathbb{P}(S_n\in (n^{1-2/m},2n^{1-2/m}))>\delta_1$.


Given that a sample point $x$ lies in $B(0,R)$, the probability that $x$ has its modulus in $[R-1, R)$ (that is, $x\in B(0, R)\backslash B(0, R-1)$) is
\begin{align*}
  p_{[R-1,R)}&=\frac{\int_{R-1}^R \frac{c_m s_{m-1}r^{m-1}}{1+r^{m+1}}\,dr }{1-p_{R}}
 % &<\frac{\int_{R-1}^R \frac{c_m s_{m-1}}{r^2}\,dr }{1-p_{R}}\\
  <-\frac{\frac{c_m s_{m-1}}{R}+\frac{c_m s_{m-1}}{R-1}}{1-\frac{c_m s_{m-1}}{R}}
  <\frac{2c_m s_{m-1}}{R^2}
\end{align*}
for sufficiently large $R$. For $R_n=\frac{2n^{2/m}}{c_m s_{m-1}}$ we have $p_{[R-1,R)}\leq n^{-2/m}$ for large $n$.

Let $\hat{S}_n$ the number of samples in $B(0, R_n)\backslash B(0, R_n-1)$ conditioned on there being $S_n$ samples outside $B(0,R_n)$. We have $\hat{S}_n \sim \mathcal{B}(n-S_n, p_{[R_n, R_n-1)})$. We can couple $\mathcal{B}(n-S_n, p_{[R_n, R_n-1)})$ to $\mathcal{B}(n, p_{[R_n, R_n-1)})$ to imply that $\mathbb{P}(\hat{S}_n\leq n^{1-2/m})\geq \frac{1}{2}$.



Consider the Rips complex at radius $1$. We want to lower bound the probability that all the points outside $B(0,R)$ are isolated. A sufficient condition for this is that there are no edges between points outside $B(0, R-1)$.

\begin{align*}
  \mathbb{P}(\|x-y\|\leq 1|\mid \|x\|, \|y\|\geq R-1)&=
  \frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(\|x\|,\|y\|\geq R-1)}\int_{R-1}^\infty \frac{c_m s_{m-1}r^{m-1}}{1+r^{m+1}} \mathbb{P}(y\in B(x,1)\mid |x|=r) \,dr
\end{align*}

If $|x|=r$ ($r$ sufficiently large), then the probability that another sample $y$ lies in $B(x,1)$ is bounded above by $\int_{B(x,1)} \frac{c_m}{1+\|z\|^{m+1}}dz \leq C/r^m$ for some constant $C$ independent of $r$. This implies that there exists a constant $C_1$ independent of $R$ such that
\begin{align*}
  \mathbb{P}(\|x-y\|\leq 1|\mid \|x\|, \|y\|>R-1)& < (R-1)^2 C_1\int_{R-1}^\infty \frac{1}{r^{m+2}}\,dr.
\end{align*}
Thus there is some constant $C_2$ independent of $R$ (for sufficiently large $R$) such that
$$
\mathbb{P}(\|x-y\|\leq 1|\mid \|x\|, \|y\|>R-1)\leq \frac{C_2}{R^{m-1}}.
$$

Let $Y_n$ be the number of edges in the Rips complex at radius $1$ between points outside $B(0,R_n-1)$. When $S_n\leq 2n^{1-2/m}$ and $\hat{S}_n\leq n^{1-2/m}$ then the number of samples outside $B(0, R-1)$ is upper bounded by $3n^{1-2/m}$. For each pair of points the probability of an edge between them is bounded above by $C_2/R_n^{m-1}$. Together we get the upper bound on expectation
\begin{align*}
  \E[Y_n|S_n\leq 2n^{1-2/m} \text{ and } \hat{S}_n\leq n^{1-2/m}]&\leq \frac{(3n^{1-2/m})^2}{2}C_2\left(\frac{c_m s_m}{2}\right)^{m-1} (n^{2/m})^{-m+1}
  =\frac{C_3}{n^{2/m}}
\end{align*}
for some $C_3$ independent of (sufficiently large) $n$.


Fix any $\delta_2\in (0,1)$. We have $\E[Y_n|S_n<2n^{1-2/m}  \text{ and } \hat{S}_n\leq n^{1-2/m}]<1-\delta_2$ for sufficiently large $n$. As $Y_n$ is a non-negative integer we get $\mathbb{P}(Y_n=0\mid S_n<2n^{1-2/m} \text{ and } \hat{S}_n\leq n^{1-2/m})>\delta_2$ for sufficiently large $n$.

Combined we have
$$
\P(S_n \in (n^{1-2/m},2n^{1-2/m}) \text{ and } \hat{S}_n\leq n^{1-2/m}\text{ and } Y_n=0)>\frac{1}{2}\delta_1\delta_2
$$
for sufficiently large $n$. When $S_n \in (n^{1-2/m},2n^{1-2/m})$ and $\hat{S}_n\leq n^{1-2/m}$ and $Y_n=0$ we are guaranteed that at least $n^{1-2/m}$ points are isolated in the Rips complex at radius $1$. This implies that there are at least $n^{1-2/m}$ connected components and $d_{n-n^{1-2/m}}\geq 1$.

If we set $\omega(n)=n-n^{1-2/m}$, then we do \emph{not} have the limit $d_{\omega(n)}\to 0$ as $n\to \infty$ with probability $1$ which means that $d_{\omega(n)}$ is not an admissible bandwidth.

\subsection{Quantile death times $d_{\gamma(n-1)}$ are an admissible bandwidth}

Despite the counterexample in the previous subsection, we can find consistent estimators using sequences of death times under very mild assumptions of the distribution (even with fat tails!).
An approach that generally works is to a percentile of the death times. That is $d_{\gamma(n-1)}$ for some $\gamma\in [0,1]$. Recall that there are only $n-1$ finite death times, so $d_{n-1}$ is the largest finite death value. Strictly speaking, we will need an integer value, so you can interpret $\gamma(n-1)$ as either the floor or the ceiling; the same limits will hold in either case.

The following is a standard useful lemma, but we include it for completeness.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:samplescover}
  Let $U_1, \ldots, U_l\subset \R^m$ and $X$ a random sample such that $\P(X\in U_i)>\delta $ for all $i$. Let $\{x_1, \ldots x_n\}$ be i.i.d. draws of $X$. If $Z_i$ is the event that at no samples $x_j$ lie in $U_i$ then
  $$
  \P(\cup Z_i) \leq l e^{-n\delta}.
  $$
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  For a single draw $x_j$ we have $\P(x_j\notin Z_i)\leq 1-\delta\leq e^{-\delta}$ and thus for $n$ draws $\P(Z_i)\leq e^{-n\delta}$. Combining over all the $Z_i$ we gain the upper bound
  $\P(\cup Z_i)\leq \sum_i \P(Z_i)\leq le^{-n\delta}$.
\end{proof}

It is useful to have notation for offsets of Euclidean subsets. For any subset $L\subset \R^m$ and any $\delta>0$, let $L^\delta$ denote the set $\{y+v \mid y\in L, \|v\| \leq \delta\}$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:covernumber}
  Let $f: \R^m\to [0,\infty)$ be a Lipschitz probability density function (with Lipschitz constant $M$), and $L=f^{-1} [c,\infty)$ be a non-empty superlevel set of $f$ for some $c>0$. For all $\varepsilon\in(0, c/2M]$ there exists $\{x_1, \ldots x_l\}\subset L$ such that $\{B(x_i, \varepsilon)\}$ is a cover of $L$, $l\leq\frac{2^{m+2}}{c b_m \varepsilon^m}$, and $\P(X\in B(x_i, \varepsilon))>\frac{c b_m}{2} \varepsilon^m$ for all $i$ (where $X$ is drawn according to pdf $f$). Here $b_m$ is the volume of the unit ball in $\R^m$.
  %Furthermore, $\cup_i B(x_i, \varepsilon)$ contains at most $$??$$
  % connected components.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Fix $\varepsilon \in (0, c/M]$ and take a maximal $(\varepsilon/2)$-separated subset $S = \{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_l\}$ within $L$. This means that $|x_i-x_j|\geq \varepsilon$ for all $i\neq j$ and that no additional point in $L$ can be added to $S$ without breaking this condition. By construction this implies that $L\subset \cup_{i} B(x_i, \varepsilon)$ as otherwise any missed location in $L$ could be added as an additional point and the set remain $(\varepsilon/2)$-separated.

  As each $x_i\in L$ we also have $B(x_i, \varepsilon/2)\subset L^{\varepsilon/2}$. Since $f$ is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant $M$, we have
  $L^{\varepsilon/2} \subseteq f^{-1}[c-M\varepsilon/2, \infty)$.
  For $\varepsilon<c/M$ we have $B(x_i, \varepsilon/2)\subset f^{-1}[c/2, \infty)$ so $$\P(X\in B(x_i, \varepsilon/2))=\int_{B(x_i, \varepsilon/2)}f dx \geq \frac{c b_m \varepsilon^m}{2^{m+2}}.$$ Each of the $B(x_i, \varepsilon/2)$ are disjoint so $l\frac{c b_m \varepsilon^m}{2^{m+2}} \leq \P(X\in \cup_i B(x_i, \varepsilon/2))\leq 1$. We thus get $l\leq \frac{2^{m+2}} {c b_m \varepsilon^d}$.

  As $B(x_i, \varepsilon) \subset f^{-1}[c-M\varepsilon, \infty)\subset f^{-1}[c/2, \infty]$ we have $\P(X\in B(x_i, \varepsilon))\geq \frac{c b_m \varepsilon^m}{2}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:d_alphan to 0}
  Let $f:\R^m \to \R$ be a Lipschitz probability density function (with Lipschitz constant $M$) for $X$. For any $\gamma\in (0,1)$, the limit $d_{\gamma (n-1)} \to 0$ converges almost surely, where $d_{\gamma (n-1)}$ is the $\gamma$ quantile of finite death times.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Fix $\gamma\in [0,1)$.
Choose $c>0$ such that for $L=f^{-1}[c, \infty)$ we have $\P(X\notin L)<(1-\gamma)/2$.

  Consider the sequence $\varepsilon_n:= \left(\frac{2\log n}{c b_m n}\right)^{1/m}$, so that
  $$
  \frac{c b_m \varepsilon_n^m}{2} =\frac{\log n}{n}.
  $$
  (where $b_m$ is the volume of the unit ball in $\R^m$).
  Note that for $\varepsilon_n \in (0, c/2M)$ for large enough $n$. Restrict the sequence to $n$ where this holds.

  By \cref{lem:covernumber} there exists a cover of $L$ by $l_n$ balls $\{B(y_i, \varepsilon_n)\mid i=1, 2, \ldots l_n\}$, with
  $$
  l_n\leq \frac{2^{m+1}}{c b_m \varepsilon_n^m}=\frac{2^m n}{\log n}
  $$
  and
  $\P(X\in B(y_i, \varepsilon_n))>\frac{c b_m \varepsilon_n^m}{2} = \frac{\log n}{n}$ for each $i$.
 %We have $d_{\gamma(n-1)}<r_n$ whenever $\cup_i B(x_i, r_n)$ has less than $n(1-\gamma)$ connected components.
  If $Z_i$ is the event that there are no samples in $B(y_i, \varepsilon)$. By \cref{lem:samplescover}
  $$
  \P(\cup Z_i) \leq \frac{2^m n}{\log n} e^{-n \frac{\log n}{n}}= \frac{2^m}{\log n} .
  $$
  This implies that as $n\to \infty$ we have at least one sample in each of the $B(y_i, \varepsilon_n)$ almost surely. All points with the same ball $B(y_i, \varepsilon_n)$ will be connected in the Rips complex with radius $2\varepsilon_n$. This implies that the number of connected components when restricting to points inside $L$ will be bounded above by $l_n \leq \frac{2^m n}{\log n}$ (almost surely).

  %Let $p=\P(X\notin L)$. Note that for all $x\in L$ we have
  % $f(x)>c$. Since $f$ has only finitely many local maxima we have
  % that the number of connected components in $L$ is finite.
  %Denote these connected components by $L_1, L_2, \ldots L_K$.
  % %Consider also the sets $L_i^\varepsilon=\{y\in \R^m| \text{ there
  % exists }x\in L_i \text{ such that } \|x-y\|<\varepsilon\}$
  %We also know that the volumes of the $L_i$ are all bounded.

  Let $Y(L,n)$ denote the random variable for the number of our $n$ sample points that are not in $L$. In the case where $\cup Z_i$ holds, we would have an upper bound on the number of connected components in $\cup_j B(x_j, \epsilon_n)$ by $
  Y(L,n)+\frac{2^m n}{\log n}$. 

  We have $d_{\gamma(n-1)}<\epsilon_n$ whenever $\cup_i B(x_i, \epsilon_n)$ has less than $n(1-\gamma)$ connected components. 
  We will want to show that
  $$
  Y(L,n)+\frac{2^m n}{\log n}<n(1-\gamma)
  $$
  for large $n$. As $\frac{2^m n}{\log n}<\frac{n(1-\gamma)}{6}$ for sufficiently large $n$, it is sufficient to show $Y(L,n)<5n(1-\gamma)/6$ for large $n$.

  From the construction of $L$ we have $Y(L,n)$ is a binomial distribution with mean $np$ and variance $np(1-p)$, with $p<(1-\gamma)/2$. We can use Chebyshev's inequality to bound $\P(Y(L,n)>5n(1-\gamma)/6)$.
  \begin{align*}
    \P(Y(L,n)>5n(1-\gamma)/6) & \leq
    \P(|Y(L,n)-np|>5n(1-\gamma)/6-n(1-\gamma)/2) \\
    & = \P(|Y(L,n)-np| >n(1-\gamma)/3)                    \\
    & \leq \frac{Var(Y)}{(n(1-\gamma)/3)^2}             \\
    & \leq \frac{9np(1-p)}{n^2(1-\gamma)^2}             \\
    %&\leq \frac{9(1-\gamma)(1-p)/2}{n(1-\gamma)^2}\\
    & \leq \frac{9}{2n(1-\gamma)}
  \end{align*}
  This clearly goes to zero as $n$ goes to infinity.

  If there are less than $n(1-\gamma)$ connected components in $\cup_j B(x_j, \varepsilon_n)$ then $d_{\gamma(n-1)}< \varepsilon_n$. Thus we have proved $$\mathbb{P}(d_{\gamma(n-1)} > 
  \varepsilon_n)\leq \P(\cup Z_i)+\P(Y(L,n)>5n(1-\gamma)/6)\to 0$$ as $n\to \infty$.
  As $\varepsilon_n \to 0$ as $n\to \infty$ we have $d_{\gamma(n-1)} \to 0$ almost surely.
\end{proof}

We can combine Theorem \ref{thm:d_alphan to 0} with Theorem \ref{thm:nd_f(n) in plane}  to show $d_{\gamma(n-1)}$ is an admissible bandwidth as a corollary.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:consistency}
  Let $f:\R^m \to \R$ be a Lipschitz probability density function for $X$. Fix $\gamma\in (0,1)$. Then $d_{\gamma (n-1)} \to 0$ (where $d_{\gamma (n-1)}$ is the $\gamma$ quantile of finite death times) is an admissible bandwidth.
\end{corollary}
