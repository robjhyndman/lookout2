\section{Bandwidth selection from the minimum spanning tree} \label{sec:mst}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{The bandwidth rule}

Let $T$ denote the Euclidean minimum spanning tree of $\{\bm{y}_1,\dots,\bm{y}_n\}\subset\R^m$: the connected graph on the $n$ observations whose total edge length is smallest. Provided the pairwise distances are distinct, which holds almost surely when the observations are drawn from a continuous density, $T$ is unique and has $n-1$ edges. When placed in increasing order, the edge lengths are denoted by $e_{(1)} \le \cdots \le e_{(n-1)}$.

\begin{defn}[Minimum spanning tree bandwidth]\label{def:mstbw}
  For $\gamma\in(0,1)$, the \emph{minimum spanning tree bandwidth} is the $\gamma$ quantile of the edge lengths of $T$,
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:mstbw}
    h_n = e_{(\lceil \gamma(n-1)\rceil)}.
  \end{equation}
\end{defn}
We take $\gamma = 0.98$ throughout unless stated otherwise. (In computation, we use the median-unbiased quantile estimator of \citet{HF96} rather than the order statistic itself, but the distinction is immaterial asymptotically.) The kernel density estimate \eqref{eq:mkde} is then computed with the bandwidth matrix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Hmst}
  \bm{H} = ah_n^2\bm{I}_m,
\end{equation}
where $a=m+4$ for the Epanechnikov kernel.

The single linkage reading of \eqref{eq:mstbw} assists interpretability. Cutting the single linkage dendrogram at height $h_n$ splits the $n$ observations into about $(1-\gamma)n$ connected components. With $\gamma=0.98$, the bandwidth is the scale at which single linkage has gathered all but about 2\% of the observations into components. It is a measure of how far apart neighbouring observations are once the few most isolated ones are set aside.

To compute $h_n$, we use the dual-tree Bor\r{u}vka algorithm of \citet{March2010}, as implemented in \texttt{mlpack} \citep{mlpackR}, which builds the exact Euclidean minimum spanning tree in $O(n\log n)$ time under the same assumptions that make dual-tree nearest-neighbour search efficient, and degrades gracefully in higher dimensions.

\subsection{Other uses of MST in anomaly detection}\label{sec:related}

Minimum spanning trees have a long history in multivariate statistics, and we are not the first to use them in an anomaly detection context.
\citet{Rohlf1975} gave the classical minimum spanning tree test for multivariate outliers. When $m=1$, the edges of the minimum spanning tree are the gaps between consecutive order statistics, so the edge lengths of the tree are the natural multivariate generalization of the univariate gap test; Rohlf tests the largest squared edge length, divided by the mean squared edge length, against a gamma null distribution. However, \citet{caroni1995rohlf} argue that this is not a useful formal test.

Related uses of the tree include single linkage detectors, which exploit the equivalence between the minimum spanning tree and single linkage clustering \citep{Gower1969} to cut the dendrogram and declare the resulting small clusters anomalous \citep{Jiang2001}, and minimum spanning tree clustering, which deletes inconsistently long edges to recover cluster structure \citep{Zahn1971}. \citet{FriedmanRafsky1979} give the broader statistical setting, using minimum spanning trees to construct multivariate analogues of the Wald-Wolfowitz and Smirnov two-sample tests.

The distinction that matters is what the tree is used for. We do not use the minimum spanning tree to label anomalies. We use the distribution of its edge lengths only to choose a scale. Labelling is done by the leave-one-out kernel density estimate and the fitted generalized Pareto distribution, exactly as in \cref{alg:lookout}. A long edge of $T$ therefore does not make its endpoint an anomaly, and an observation can be anomalous without being incident to a long edge.

\subsection{The order of the quantile edge length}\label{sec:order}

This section establishes the order of $h_n = e_{(\lceil\gamma(n-1)\rceil)}$, shows why a fixed quantile is the right thing to take and the largest gap is not, and works out what the resulting bandwidth does to the kernel density estimate. Throughout, $b_m$ denotes the volume of the unit ball in $\R^m$ and $\{\omega_n\}$ a sequence of integers indexing which edge length is used. Proofs of \cref{prop:order} and of \cref{cex:fattails} are in \cref{app:proofs}.

The quantile is easiest to study through the single linkage reading of \eqref{eq:mstbw}. For $r>0$, let $C_n(r)$ be the number of connected components of the graph that joins every pair of observations at distance at most $r$. Kruskal's algorithm builds $T$ by adding edges in increasing order of length, so the edges of $T$ of length at most $r$ form a spanning forest of that graph, and there are $n-C_n(r)$ of them. Hence $h_n\le r$ exactly when $C_n(r)\le n-\lceil\gamma(n-1)\rceil$, and questions about $h_n$ become questions about a component count.

The number of components of a random geometric graph obeys a law of large numbers, with a limit expressed through a Poisson process. For $\lambda>0$, let $\mathcal{P}_\lambda$ be a homogeneous Poisson process of intensity $\lambda$ on $\R^m$, join each pair of points of $\mathcal{P}_\lambda\cup\{\bm{0}\}$ at distance at most $1$, let $\mathcal{C}_\lambda$ be the component containing the origin, and put $\kappa(\lambda)=\E[1/|\mathcal{C}_\lambda|]$, with $1/|\mathcal{C}_\lambda|=0$ when the component is infinite. Thus $\kappa(\lambda)$ is the number of components per point when points of intensity $\lambda$ are joined at unit distance.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:order}
  Let $\bm{y}_1,\dots,\bm{y}_n$ be sampled independently from a density $f$ on $\R^m$, and fix $\gamma\in(0,1)$. Then $n^{1/m}h_n\to q_\gamma(f)$ in probability as $n\to\infty$, where $q_\gamma(f)\in(0,\infty)$ is the unique solution $q$ of
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:qgamma}
    \int_{\R^m}\kappa\big(q^m f(\bm{x})\big)f(\bm{x})\,d\bm{x}=1-\gamma.
  \end{equation}
  Moreover $t_-\le q_\gamma(f)\le t_+$, where $t_-$ and $t_+$ are the unique positive solutions $t$ of
  $$
  \int_{\R^m}e^{-b_m t^m f(\bm{x})}f(\bm{x})\,d\bm{x}=1-\gamma
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \int_{\R^m}\big(1-e^{-b_m t^m f(\bm{x})}\big)\,d\bm{x}=(1-\gamma)\,b_m t^m
  $$
  respectively.
\end{proposition}

Nothing is assumed about $f$: no smoothness, no bound, and no condition on the tails. The proposition therefore covers the heavy-tailed density of \cref{sec:counterexample} as well as every light-tailed one. It rests on the weak law of large numbers of \citet{PenroseYukich2003} for sums of locally determined contributions from random point sets, and \eqref{eq:qgamma} is the single linkage reading carried to the limit: once distances are rescaled by $n^{1/m}$, $q_\gamma(f)$ is the scale at which a large sample from $f$ has $1-\gamma$ components per observation. The price of that generality is that the proposition gives a limit without a rate, and no probability bound at any fixed $n$.

The function $\kappa$ has no closed form, and $t_-$ and $t_+$ come from two elementary bounds on it. The origin is a component on its own with probability $e^{-b_m\lambda}$, so $\kappa(\lambda)\ge e^{-b_m\lambda}$. Its component contains all of its neighbours, whose number is Poisson with mean $b_m\lambda$, so $\kappa(\lambda)\le(1-e^{-b_m\lambda})/(b_m\lambda)$. The resulting bounds are explicit and reasonably tight. With $\gamma=0.98$, the uniform density on $[0,1]^2$ gives $t_-=1.12$ and $t_+=3.99$, against $n^{1/2}h_n\approx1.24$ in simulation at $n=64{,}000$, and the bivariate standard normal gives $t_-=10.0$ and $t_+=25.2$, against about $12.5$.

\begin{remark}[Nearest-neighbour distances]\label{rem:nn}
  It is natural to ask why the tree is needed when nearest-neighbour distances have the same $n^{-1/m}$ scaling and are cheaper still. The two are not alternatives: by the cut property, the edge from an observation to its nearest neighbour is the unique shortest edge across the cut isolating that observation, so it belongs to the minimum spanning tree, and every nearest-neighbour distance is an edge length of $T$. The comparison also runs the other way. A component of the graph at scale $r$ with $k$ observations accounts for $k-1$ of the $n-C_n(r)$ edges of $T$ of length at most $r$, and only components with more than one observation contribute, so there are no more such edges than there are observations whose nearest neighbour lies within $r$. Writing $D_{(1)}\le\cdots\le D_{(n)}$ for the ordered nearest-neighbour distances, it follows that $h_n\ge D_{(\lceil\gamma(n-1)\rceil)}$ for every sample. The lower bound $t_-$ in \cref{prop:order} is the limit of $n^{1/m}D_{(\lceil\gamma(n-1)\rceil)}$. The gap between $t_-$ and $q_\gamma(f)$ reflects the components with more than one observation, and that is what the tree adds: a record of how the observations join up rather than only of how close each is to its closest companion. \cref{sec:experiments} compares lookout against detectors built directly on nearest-neighbour distances.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The longest edge]\label{rem:penrose}
  The results above say nothing about $e_{(n-1)}$, the longest edge, which is the object a max-gap rule is closest to. Its behaviour is known in considerable detail: \citet{Penrose1997} gives the limiting distribution of the longest edge of the minimum spanning tree for densities on a compact region, and \citet{Penrose1998} treats the normally distributed case. Both are sharper than anything we prove or need, and we refer the reader to them rather than restating weaker versions here.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Why a fixed quantile and not the largest gap}\label{sec:counterexample}

The original bandwidth rule takes the largest gap in the upper half of the edge lengths, which places it at the extreme top of the edge-length distribution, where $\omega_n/n\to1$. \cref{prop:order} does not apply there, and for good reason: at heavy tails the top of the distribution need not settle down at all.

\begin{counterexample}\label{cex:fattails}
  Let $m\geq3$ and let the observations be sampled from the density $f(\bm{x})=c_m/(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{m+1})$ on $\R^m$. For $\omega_n=n-\tfrac{1}{4}n^{1-2/m}$ we have $\P(e_{(\omega_n)}>1)>1/4$ for all sufficiently large $n$, so $e_{(\omega_n)}$ does not converge to zero.
\end{counterexample}

The mechanism is crackle \citep{Crackle}. Under a heavy-tailed density the sample has, with probability bounded away from zero, a halo of order $n^{1-2/m}$ points at radius beyond $R_n \asymp n^{2/m}$ that are mutually isolated at unit scale, each forming its own connected component. Any rule that reads the top $O(n^{1-2/m})$ of the edge-length distribution is reading that halo, and the halo does not shrink. A fixed quantile $\gamma<1$ never looks that far up, and \cref{prop:order} shows it converges at the right rate for this density as for any other. The full argument is in \cref{app:proofs}.

There is a second way to see where the difficulty lives. \citet{Steele1988} showed for compactly supported densities, and \citet[Theorem 1.1]{PenroseYukich2003} for densities on $\R^m$ satisfying a moment condition, that the total length $M_n$ of the minimum spanning tree satisfies $n^{1/m-1}M_n\to C(1,m)\int f^{1-1/m}$ almost surely; the proof is given in \citet{Yukich1998}. The integral $\int f^{1-1/m}$ is finite for compactly supported and Gaussian densities, but for the density above $f^{1-1/m}$ decays like $\|\bm{x}\|^{-(m^2-1)/m}$ and $(m^2-1)/m<m$, so the integral diverges and the total tree length grows faster than $n^{1-1/m}$. The excess is absorbed entirely by the longest edges: the $\gamma$ quantile still behaves like $n^{-1/m}$ while the mean edge length does not. The same integral that controls the scaling is the one that fails under heavy tails, and it fails exactly where the max-gap rule looks and the fixed quantile does not.

This is the formal version of the objection raised in \cref{sec:related}. \citet{Rohlf1975} reads the same upper tail to find outliers, and refers it to the mean edge length; for this density neither quantity settles down, while the $\gamma$ quantile does.

\subsection{The bandwidth sits at the critical scaling}\label{sec:critical}

We can now say what \eqref{eq:Hmst} does to the kernel density estimate.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:critical}
  Let $f$ be a density on $\R^m$ and let $\bm{H}=(m+4)h_n^2\bm{I}_m$ with $h_n=e_{(\lceil\gamma(n-1)\rceil)}$. Then $n|\bm{H}|^{1/2}\to(m+4)^{m/2}q_\gamma(f)^m$ in probability as $n\to\infty$, and the limit is finite and positive.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  $|\bm{H}|^{1/2}=(m+4)^{m/2}h_n^m$, so $n|\bm{H}|^{1/2}=(m+4)^{m/2}(n^{1/m}h_n)^m$, and \cref{prop:order} gives $n^{1/m}h_n\to q_\gamma(f)\in(0,\infty)$ in probability.
\end{proof}

Condition A3 therefore fails: $n|\bm{H}|^{1/2}$ is bounded rather than divergent, the variance term $n^{-1}|\bm{H}|^{-1/2}R(K)$ of the MISE does not vanish, and the estimator is not MISE-consistent. This is a property of the rule, not an oversight, and it has a clean reading. The expected number of observations falling inside the kernel window at a typical point is of order $n|\bm{H}|^{1/2}f$, which \cref{prop:critical} says is $O(1)$ in $n$: the estimator has a fixed local resolution, set by a fixed number of neighbours rather than by a shrinking neighbourhood. That is the same design as a fixed-$k$ nearest-neighbour density estimate, which is likewise inconsistent and which underlies both the local outlier factor \citep{breunig2000lof} and nearest-neighbour outlier scores. \cref{sec:whynotmise} argues that this is the right place to be.

\begin{remark}[Consistency is one factor away]\label{rem:inflate}
  Nothing prevents consistency if it is wanted. For any $a_n\to\infty$, however slowly, the inflated bandwidth $a_nh_n$ satisfies $a_nh_n\to0$ and $n(a_nh_n)^m\asymp a_n^m\to\infty$, so it is an admissible bandwidth in the sense of \cref{def:admissible_bandwidth} and the resulting estimator is MISE-consistent. Taking $a_n=\log n$ changes the bandwidth by a factor invisible at the sample sizes considered here. The rule sits at the boundary by choice.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Why MISE-optimality is the wrong target}\label{sec:whynotmise}

An asymptotic approximation to the MISE \eqref{eq:mise} is given by \citet{chacon2018multivariate}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:amse}
  \text{AMISE}(\hat{f}) = n^{-1}|\bm{H}|^{-1/2} R(K) + \frac{1}{4} \mu_2(K)^2 \int \big\{\text{tr}(\bm{H} D^2 f(\bm{u}))\big\}^2 d\bm{u},
\end{equation}
where $R(K) = \int K(\bm{u})^2 d\bm{u}$ and $D^2 f$ is the Hessian of $f$. Writing $\bm{H}=h^2\bm{I}_m$ with $h$ a length, this is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:amse_iso}
  \text{AMISE} = n^{-1} h^{-m} R(K) + \frac{1}{4} \mu_2(K)^2 h^4 \int \big\{\Delta f(\bm{u})\big\}^2 d\bm{u},
\end{equation}
minimised at $h_{\text{opt}} \asymp n^{-1/(m+4)}$, with MISE of order $n^{-4/(m+4)}$. Against this, \cref{prop:order} gives $h_n \asymp n^{-1/m}$. The ratio is
$$
\frac{h_n}{h_{\text{opt}}} \asymp n^{-4/\{m(m+4)\}},
$$
which tends to zero for every $m\geq1$. The minimum spanning tree bandwidth undersmooths relative to MISE in every dimension, severely when $m$ is small and mildly when $m$ is large. At $h\asymp n^{-1/m}$ the bias term of \eqref{eq:amse_iso} is $O(n^{-4/m})$ and vanishes, while the variance term is $\Theta(1)$; all of the residual error is variance.

We argue that this is acceptable, and for anomaly detection desirable. The MISE is an integrated criterion weighted by the density itself, so it is dominated by the bulk of the distribution, and the bandwidth that minimises it is the one that best describes where most of the data are. Anomaly detection asks a different question. What matters is the ranking of $\hat f$ at the observed points in the low-density region, and the upper tail of the surprisals $-\log\hat f(\bm{y}_i)$, neither of which is what \eqref{eq:amse} measures. A bandwidth chosen for the bulk oversmooths the tail, filling in the gaps around isolated observations with probability mass borrowed from their distant neighbours, which is precisely the signal an anomaly detector needs to keep. Undersmoothing keeps isolated points isolated.

The guarantee to demand is therefore not consistency and not an optimal rate, but the correct order: $h_n$ should be the scale at which neighbouring observations sit, which is $n^{-1/m}$. That is what \cref{prop:order} delivers, and it is what rules out the largest-gap rule, whose bandwidth fails even to converge to zero under the heavy tails of \cref{cex:fattails}. Correct order is a weaker requirement than MISE-optimality, and it is the one that discriminates between the two rules.
