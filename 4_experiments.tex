\section{Numerical experiments}\label{sec:experiments}
% -------------------------------------------------------------------------

We now evaluate the new version of lookout in five simulation experiments. Experiments 1 and 2 compare it against the original version, Experiments 3 to 5 compare both versions with four other anomaly detection methods, and \cref{sec:bwablation} examines the sensitivity of the results to the bandwidth. Two further comparisons of the two versions, and a set of showcase examples, are in the online Appendix. Both versions are run in the current implementation, version 2.1.0 of the \texttt{lookout} package \citep{Rlookout}. The original version uses min-max scaling, the largest-gap bandwidth of \cref{alg:lookout} with the median restriction, and an unconstrained GPD fit, and is otherwise identical to \cref{alg:modifiedlookout}: in particular, it uses $\bm{H}=(m+4)d_*^2\bm{I}_m$ with the kernel of \cref{alg:modifiedlookout}, so that $d_*$ plays the role of $h_n$, and the same $\alpha$, $\beta$ and probability $p_i$. The differences between the two versions are therefore due only to the three modifications of \cref{sec:modified}.

The four other methods are HDoutliers \citep{wilkinson2017visualizing} and \textit{stray} \citep{stray}, which apply extreme value theory to nearest-neighbour distances and are run with their $\alpha$ parameter set to 0.01, the default for stray, and KDEOS \citep{Schubert2014} and RDOS \citep{Tang2017}, which are based on kernel density estimates and are run with their default settings. The same four methods were used for comparison by \citet{lookout}. Experiments 1 and 2 report the true positive and false negative rates of the two versions of lookout. HDoutliers and stray return labels, so in Experiments 3 to 5 we compare them with lookout by the Fmeasure and Gmean,
\[
  \text{Fmeasure} = \frac{2\,\text{Precision}\times\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}},
  \qquad
  \text{Gmean} = \sqrt{\text{Sensitivity} \times \text{Specificity}},
\]
where Precision $= \text{TP}/(\text{TP} + \text{FP})$, Recall $=$ Sensitivity $= \text{TP}/(\text{TP} + \text{FN})$ and Specificity $= \text{TN}/(\text{TN} + \text{FP})$, with TP, FP, TN and FN the numbers of true positives, false positives, true negatives and false negatives. KDEOS and RDOS return anomaly scores, so we compare them with the scores $p_i$ of lookout by the area under the ROC curve (AUC), which plots sensitivity against one minus specificity over all thresholds.

\subsection{Experiment 1: Different anomaly rates for Gamma distribution}

The non-anomalous observations have independent $\text{Gamma}(2, 2)$ coordinates in $\R^2$, and the anomalies independent $\text{Gamma}(2, r)$ coordinates with $r \in \{0.1, 0.2, \ldots, 1\}$. Each replication has 500 non-anomalies and 10 anomalies, and there are 10 replications for each value of $r$.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figures/Exp1_Gamma_Rates.pdf}
  \caption{Experiment 1 data and results. Left: data for two iterations of the experiment with $r = 0.2$ and $r = 0.9$. Anomalies with lower values of $r$ are easier to identify. Right: the relative performance of the two algorithms. The points in the right panel are slightly jittered horizontally to avoid overlapping points.}
  \label{fig:exp1}
\end{figure}

\cref{fig:exp1} shows the data and results. As $r$ increases the anomalies become more similar to the non-anomalies, so the true positive rate falls. The new version performs better than the original at every value of $r$, with the largest differences at the smallest values of $r$, where the anomalies are farthest from the non-anomalous observations.

\subsection[Experiment 2: Anomalies on the boundary as n increases]{Experiment 2: Anomalies on the boundary as $n$ increases}

Anomalies are often close to the non-anomalous observations rather than well separated from them. Here the non-anomalous observations have independent $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ coordinates in $\R^2$, with $n \in \{1000, 2000, \ldots, 10000\}$, and there are $0.005n$ anomalies in a ring just beyond most of them. For each anomaly we sample $\mu_{i1} \sim \mathcal{U}(-2.2\sqrt2, 2.2\sqrt2)$, set $\mu_{i2} = \pm\sqrt{2\times2.2^2 - \mu_{i1}^2}$ with the negative sign for a randomly chosen half of the anomalies, so that the anomaly means lie on a circle of radius $2.2\sqrt2$ about the origin, and draw $Y_{i1} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{i1}, 0.1)$ and $Y_{i2} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{i2}, 0.1)$. There are 10 replications for each $n$, and for both versions the kernel density estimates use the truncated sum described in \cref{sec:modified}, with 100 neighbours.

\cref{fig:exp2} shows the data of one replication and the results. Both versions have low false positive rates. The new version has a higher true positive rate at the smaller sample sizes, and the two are similar beyond about $n=5000$. The online Appendix repeats this experiment with gamma-distributed observations (Experiment S2); there the two versions differ little, and the original is slightly better at the largest sample sizes.

\subsection{Experiment 3: Normal distributions moving out in each iteration}

The sample has 405 points in $\R^6$, of which five are anomalies. The non-anomalies are $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ in every dimension. The anomalies differ from the non-anomalies only in the first dimension, where they are $\mathcal{N}\left(2 + (i-1)\times0.5, \, 0.2 \right)$ at iteration $i \in \{1, \ldots, 10\}$, so they move further from the non-anomalous points at each iteration. Each iteration is repeated 10 times.

\cref{fig:exp3} shows the data and the results, with anomalies in red. The top left plot shows the $Y_1Y_2$ plane at the third iteration, and the middle plots the $Y_1Y_2$ and $Y_3Y_4$ planes at the ninth, by which time the anomalies have separated from the other points in the $Y_1$ direction but remain mixed with them on the $Y_3Y_4$ plane. The right panel gives the AUC, Fmeasure and Gmean. The new version has higher Fmeasure and Gmean than the original from about the third iteration, the two versions have the same AUC, and both are at least as good as the four other methods on every metric.

\subsection[Experiment 4: Annulus in R3]{Experiment 4: Annulus in $\R^3$}

The sample has 805 points in $\R^3$, of which five are anomalies. The non-anomalies lie in an annulus on the $Y_1Y_2$ plane with $Y_3 \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$, and the anomalies move towards the centre of the hole over 10 iterations, each repeated 10 times. \cref{fig:exp4} shows the data at iterations 3 and 9 and the results. The new version identifies all five anomalies from the fifth iteration, with AUC, Fmeasure and Gmean equal to one. The original version is lower and more variable, KDEOS and RDOS have AUC of at most about 0.6 throughout, and HDoutliers and stray identify no anomalies until about the eighth iteration.

\subsection[Experiment 5: Unit cube in R20]{Experiment 5: Unit cube in $\R^{20}$}

The sample has 500 points in $\R^{20}$, of which one is an anomaly. Initially all 500 points are uniform on $(0,1)$ in each dimension. At iteration $i \in \{1, \dots, 20\}$ the first $i$ coordinates of the anomaly are set to $0.9$, so that at iteration 20 it is $(0.9, \dots, 0.9)$, and each iteration is repeated 10 times. The anomaly is not anomalous in any single dimension or on any $Y_iY_j$ plane, nor in the space of the first two principal components. To display it we use \textit{dobin} \citep{dobinPaper}, a dimension reduction method designed for anomalies. \cref{fig:exp5} shows the $D_1D_2$ plane of the dobin projection at iterations 5, 12 and 20, where the anomaly is clearly separated only at the last, together with the results. Both versions of lookout are run without scaling, and they perform identically. Their AUC reaches one by about the twelfth iteration, as does that of RDOS, with KDEOS a little lower. Gmean reaches one soon after; Fmeasure stays below $0.5$, because a single false positive halves it when there is one anomaly. HDoutliers and stray never identify the anomaly.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the bandwidth}\label{sec:bwablation}

\cref{sec:whynotmise} argues that the bandwidth should be of the order of the spacing between observations in the sparse part of the sample, and that neither the constant nor MISE-optimality matters much. To check this, we repeat Experiments 1, 2 and 3, and Experiment S1 of the online Appendix, with the bandwidth $h_n$ multiplied by $1/4$, $1/2$, $2$ and $4$, and with $h_n$ replaced by the bandwidth $h_{\text{opt}}$ of \cref{tab:bwconst}, which is the AMISE-optimal bandwidth for the Epanechnikov kernel and a standard normal density, computed for the standardized data. Everything else in \cref{alg:modifiedlookout} is unchanged. In Experiment 2 we use $n\in\{1000, 2000, 4000\}$ and the exact leave-one-out estimate rather than the truncated sum described in \cref{sec:modified}, so that the results at large multiples of $h_n$ are not affected by the truncation. Each setting is replicated ten times. \cref{fig:ablation} reports the area under the ROC curve computed from the anomaly scores, and the true and false positive rates at $\alpha=0.01$.

The ranking of the observations is insensitive to the bandwidth. The area under the ROC curve is almost the same for every bandwidth from $h_n/2$ to $4h_n$ in all four experiments, a range of a factor of eight, and falls at $h_n/4$ in Experiments 2, 3 and S1. The threshold is more sensitive. At $h_n$ the false positive rate is between $0.005$ and $0.013$ in the four experiments, close to the nominal $\alpha=0.01$. Halving the bandwidth roughly doubles it, and at $h_n/4$ it is between $5\%$ and $27\%$, because observations with no other observation inside the kernel support have a leave-one-out estimate of zero and are all declared anomalous. Doubling or quadrupling the bandwidth lowers the false positive rate below the nominal level and, in Experiments 1 and 2, lowers the true positive rate as well, since the estimate at an isolated observation then includes contributions from the bulk of the sample. In Experiments 2 and S1, where the non-anomalous observations are normal in two dimensions, $h_{\text{opt}}$ is about $0.95h_n$ and $0.7h_n$ respectively, and gives results similar to $h_n$. In Experiment 1, where the observations are gamma distributed, and in Experiment 3, where $m=6$, $h_{\text{opt}}$ is about a quarter of $h_n$. In Experiment 1 its false positive rate is $2\%$. In Experiment 3 the GPD could not be fitted in 73 of the 100 runs, because more than $10\%$ of the observations had no other observation inside the kernel support, and in the remaining runs the false positive rate averaged $0.34$. This is the behaviour described in \cref{sec:whynotmise}: in moderate dimensions a bandwidth chosen for the bulk of the distribution is too small for the tails.
