\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Anomaly detection is used in many application domains such as credit card fraud and scam detection \citep{kolupuri2025scams}.  \cite{lookout} proposed the \textit{lookout} algorithm for anomaly detection using a kernel density estimate with bandwidth based on persistent homology. Anomalies are identified by fitting a generalized Pareto distribution (GPD) to the negative logarithm of the smallest leave-one-out kernel density estimates, and declaring points with low GPD probabilities as anomalies.

In this paper we study the bandwidth of the lookout algorithm and what it implies for the kernel density estimates. The bandwidth was originally expressed in terms of the death diameters of a Vietoris-Rips filtration; these are the edge lengths of the Euclidean minimum spanning tree of the data, and we work with the tree throughout. This allows us to analyse the asymptotic behaviour of the bandwidth, and to propose a modified algorithm that takes its bandwidth from a different feature of the same edge lengths. The modified algorithm is more robust than the original and improves on its performance in most cases.

\subsection{Anomalies and surprisals}

There is no broadly accepted definition for an anomaly. \citet{hawkins1980identification} defines an anomaly or outlier as \textit{an observation which deviates so much from other observations as to arouse suspicion it was generated by a different mechanism.} This definition implies that anomalies are rare and different from other observations. In practice, this means fitting a probability distribution to the observations and asking whether each observation is a likely draw from it. \citet{surprisals} proposed a formalization of this concept, which we state in the following definition. Throughout, $f$ is a probability density function on $\R^m$ with respect to Lebesgue measure. It is the density against which the observations are judged. We do not assume that every observation is a draw from $f$; the anomalies are the observations that are unlikely under $f$.

\begin{defn}[Anomalies]
  Given a set of observations $\bm{y}_{i} \in \R^m$ for $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$, and a probability density function $f$, we define observation $\bm{y}_i$ as anomalous if
  \begin{equation}
    \P(f(\bm{Y}) \le f(\bm{y}_{i})) < \alpha,
  \end{equation}
  where $\bm{Y}$ is a random vector with density $f$, and $\alpha>0$ is a chosen threshold.
\end{defn}

\begin{defn}[Surprisals]
  Given a probability density function $f$, the \emph{surprisal} of an observation $\bm{y}$ with respect to $f$ is defined as $-\log f(\bm{y})$.
\end{defn}
These are better known as  ``log scores'' in statistics \citep{Good1952-zw}, but we prefer the term ``surprisals'' from information theory \citep{Stone2022}, as it provides a measure of how surprising an observation is, given the probability density function $f$. Specifically, it takes large values for observations that are unlikely under $f$.

Let $S = -\log f(\bm{Y})$ be the random variable representing the surprisal of a random vector $\bm{Y}$ with density $f$, and let $G(s) = \P(S\le s)$ be its distribution function. Then the surprisal probability of observation $\bm{y}_i$ is
$$p_i = \P(S \ge s_i) = 1 - G(s_i^-),$$
where $s_i = -\log f (\bm{y}_i)$, and $G(s_i^-) = \lim_{t \uparrow s_i} G(t)$ is the left limit of $G$ at $s_i$. An observation is an anomaly if and only if $p_i < \alpha$.

\subsection{Extreme value theory}

The lookout algorithm determines which surprisals are unusually large by using a peaks-over-threshold approach \citep{Pickands1975,coles2001introduction}, and fitting a GPD to the upper tail of the observed surprisals. That is,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:POT4}
  \P(S \le s \mid S > u) = \frac{G(s) - G(u)}{1 - G(u)}
  \approx 1 - (1 + \xi (s-u)/\sigma_u)^{-1/\xi}, \qquad s > u, \quad 1 + \xi (s-u)/\sigma_u > 0,
\end{equation}
where $u$ is a threshold chosen to be large enough that the conditional distribution of the excess $S-u$ over the threshold is well approximated by a GPD, $\sigma_u > 0$ is a scale parameter depending on the threshold, and $\xi \in \R$ is a shape parameter. The case $\xi = 0$ is read as the limiting case $1 - \exp(-(s-u)/\sigma_u)$.

\subsection{Kernel density estimation}

Given a set of observations $\bm{y}_{i} \in \R^m$ for $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$, a kernel density estimate is defined as \citep{chacon2018multivariate}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mkde}
  \hat{f}(\bm{y}) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n |\bm{H}|^{-1/2} K\big(\bm{H}^{-1/2}(\bm{y} - \bm{y}_i)\big),
\end{equation}
where $K$ is a kernel function and $\bm{H}$ is a symmetric $m\times m$ positive-definite bandwidth matrix. When the observations are $n$ independent draws from $f$, the accuracy of $\hat{f}$ is conventionally measured by the mean integrated squared error (MISE),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mise}
  \text{MISE}(\hat{f}) = \E\left[\int \big(\hat{f}(\bm{u}) - f(\bm{u})\big)^2 d\bm{u}\right],
\end{equation}
where the expectation is taken over that sample, and $\hat{f}$ is a \emph{consistent estimator} of $f$ if $\text{MISE}(\hat{f})\rightarrow 0$ as $n\rightarrow\infty$. We consider matrices of the form $\bm{H} = a h_n^2 \bm{I}_m$ for a fixed constant $a>0$, where $h_n$ is a length scale computed from the sample, so that $|\bm{H}|^{1/2}=a^{m/2}h_n^m$.

For such a bandwidth matrix, $\hat{f}$ is a consistent estimator of $f$ whenever
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hzero}
  h_n \to 0
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nhinf}
  nh_n^m \to \infty
\end{equation}
in probability as $n\to\infty$, provided that $f$ is square integrable and twice differentiable with bounded, continuous and square integrable second derivatives, and $K$ is a square integrable, spherically symmetric density with a finite second moment \citep[pp.~31--37]{chacon2018multivariate}. Neither version of the lookout algorithm has a bandwidth satisfying both conditions in general: under heavy tails the original bandwidth need not satisfy \eqref{eq:hzero}, and the modified bandwidth never satisfies \eqref{eq:nhinf}. In \cref{sec:whynotmise} we argue that a consistent estimate of $f$ is not required for anomaly detection, and that the second behaviour is the appropriate one.

\subsection{The lookout algorithm for anomaly detection}

The \textit{lookout} algorithm of \cite{lookout}, as implemented in the \texttt{lookout} package, has four inputs: the dataset $\{\bm{y}_1,\dots,\bm{y}_n\}$, where each $\bm{y}_i \in \R^m$; a parameter $\beta \in (0,1)$ such that the GPD is fitted to the largest $1-\beta$ of the surprisals, with $\beta = 0.9$ following \citet{bommier2014peaks}; a parameter $\alpha \in (0,1)$ signifying the cut-off threshold for determining an anomaly; and a logical variable \textit{unitize} indicating whether to scale the data. We briefly outline the steps below.

\begin{algorithm}
  \caption{Lookout algorithm}\label{alg:lookout}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require Data $\{\bm{y}_1, \dots, \bm{y}_n\}$, proportion $\beta \in (0,1)$, threshold $\alpha \in (0,1)$, \emph{unitize} $\in \{\text{True}, \text{False}\}$.
    \State If \textit{unitize}, scale the data to lie in the unit cube in $\R^m$.
    \State Compute the persistent homology barcode of the (possibly scaled) data cloud for dimension zero using the Vietoris-Rips diameter \citep{ghrist2008barcodes}.
    \State From the barcode, obtain the ordered finite death diameters $\{d_i\}_{i = 1}^{n-1}$ for connected components, and their successive differences $\Delta d_i = d_{i+1} - d_i$.
    \State Let $d_* = d_{i_*}$ where $i_* = \text{arg\! max} \{\Delta d_i\}_{i=1}^{n-2}$; that is, $d_*$ is the diameter corresponding to the lower point of the maximum $\Delta d_i$.
    \State Compute the kernel density estimates at the observations: $f_i = \hat{f}(\bm{y}_i)$, $i=1,\dots,n$, where $\hat{f}$ is given by \eqref{eq:mkde} with the kernel $K(\bm{u}) = \frac{m+2}{2\cdot5^{m/2}b_m}(1 - \|\bm{u}\|^2/5)_+$, $b_m$ being the volume of the unit ball in $\R^m$, and $\bm{H} = d_*^2\bm{I}_m$, so that the kernel has support radius $\sqrt5\,d_*$.
    \State Compute the leave-one-out estimates $f_{-i} = \frac{1}{n-1} \big( n f_i - |\bm{H}|^{-1/2} K(\bm{0}) \big)$ for $i=1,\dots,n$.
    \State Fit a GPD to the largest $1-\beta$ of the surprisals $\{-\log f_i\}_{i=1}^n$, with location $\mu$ equal to the $\beta$ sample quantile of the surprisals, obtaining estimates of the scale and shape parameters $(\sigma, \xi)$.
    \State Calculate the probability of each observation by applying the estimated GPD to the leave-one-out estimates: $p_i = (1-\beta)\big(1 - \hat{F}(-\log f_{-i})\big)$, where $\hat{F}$ is the distribution function of the fitted GPD.
    \State If $p_i < \alpha$, declare $\bm{y}_i$ anomalous.
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Two details of \cref{alg:lookout} differ from the published description. From version 0.1.4 of the \texttt{lookout} package, the search in step 4 is restricted to the death diameters at or above their median, so that a large gap among the shortest diameters is not chosen. Equation (5) of \citet{lookout} gives the bandwidth matrix as $d_*^{2/m}\bm{I}_m$, whereas the implementation uses $d_*^2\bm{I}_m$ in every dimension, as in step 5.

The lookout algorithm provides density-based anomaly detection, where anomalies are identified as points with low density in the space of the data. If there are no true anomalies in the data, the algorithm should identify a proportion $\alpha$ of the data as false positive ``anomalies''.

Steps 2--4 of \cref{alg:lookout} can be expressed in terms of the Euclidean minimum spanning tree $T$ of the data. Components of the Vietoris-Rips complex merge when the shortest edge between them appears, which is Kruskal's algorithm \citep{Kruskal1956}, so the merges occur along the edges of $T$. The finite death diameters $d_1 \le \cdots \le d_{n-1}$ are therefore the ordered edge lengths $e_{(1)} \le \cdots \le e_{(n-1)}$ of $T$ \citep[Remark 1]{AdamsPHdim}, and also the merge heights of the single linkage dendrogram \citep{Gower1969}. In the rest of the paper we use the tree rather than the filtration, so that we can apply the limit theory for minimum spanning trees.

While \citet{lookout} demonstrated excellent results in applying their algorithm to a variety of simulated and real datasets, we see several potential areas for improvement that we aim to address in this paper. First, elements of the algorithm are not robust to outliers, including the min-max scaling, and the use of the largest gap in the Rips death diameters (equivalently, edge lengths). Second, the diagonal bandwidth matrix $\bm{H}$ does not account for correlations between variables, which can lead to suboptimal performance if the data have strong correlations. Third, the behaviour of $d_*$ as $n$ grows is not known: it is not obvious that it shrinks to zero as in \eqref{eq:hzero}, particularly for distributions with heavy tails. Finally, the GPD estimation made minimal assumptions about the distribution of the surprisal values, but we know that these are bounded above and below, and that fact can be used to improve the estimated GPD.

\subsection{The modified lookout algorithm}

As a result of these considerations, we propose three modifications to the lookout algorithm, described in detail in \cref{sec:modified}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Rather than use a min-max scaling of the data, we standardize the data using a robust covariance estimator, so that the transformed data have approximately uncorrelated coordinates with unit variance.
  \item Rather than setting the bandwidth using the edge length at the lower end of the largest gap between consecutive minimum spanning tree edge lengths, we set it using an upper quantile of those edge lengths. This avoids the problem that a few anomalies at the top of the edge length distribution can determine the largest gap and hence the bandwidth. It also gives the bandwidth the order $n^{-1/m}$ of the spacing between neighbouring observations, for any density, at which $nh_n^m$ stays bounded and \eqref{eq:nhinf} fails.
  \item The estimated GPD shape parameter is constrained to be non-positive, which improves the stability of the GPD fitting.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Other uses of the minimum spanning tree}\label{sec:related}

Minimum spanning trees have been used for anomaly detection for many years.
\citet{Rohlf1975} gave the classical minimum spanning tree test for multivariate outliers. When $m=1$, the edges of the minimum spanning tree are the gaps between consecutive order statistics, so the edge lengths of the tree generalize the gaps of the univariate gap test; Rohlf tests the largest squared edge length, divided by the mean squared edge length, with critical values derived from a gamma distribution for the squared edge lengths. However, \citet{caroni1995rohlf} argue that this is not a useful formal test.

Related uses of the tree include single linkage detectors, which exploit the equivalence between the minimum spanning tree and single linkage clustering \citep{Gower1969} to cut the dendrogram and declare the resulting small clusters anomalous \citep{Jiang2001}. \citet{Juszczak2009} build a one-class classifier from the tree of a training sample, and \citet{Ahmed2019} replace Euclidean distances by distances along the tree in a local outlier score. These detectors identify anomalies from the tree. We use it only to choose the bandwidth, and anomalies are identified by the leave-one-out kernel density estimate and the fitted generalized Pareto distribution, as in \cref{alg:lookout}, so a long edge of $T$ does not by itself make its endpoint an anomaly, and an observation can be anomalous without being incident to a long edge.

The tree has also been used to choose the bandwidth of a kernel density estimate, with the aim of estimating the density over its whole support. \citet{Chaudhuri1996} show that the resulting estimate with a uniform kernel is asymptotically unbiased on bounded sets, and \citet{SreevaniMurthy2016} take the bandwidth to be a function of the total length of the tree, with outlier rejection among the applications. The total length is determined by the bulk of the data. In contrast, we choose our bandwidth for estimating the density at isolated observations (\cref{sec:whynotmise}), and our upper quantile of the edge lengths is determined by the sparsest part of the sample.

\subsection{Outline of the paper}

In \cref{sec:mst} we define the bandwidth as a quantile of the edge lengths of the minimum spanning tree, establish its order for any density, and show that the longest edges, which the original algorithm typically uses in its bandwidth, can grow without bound. \cref{sec:modified} gives the modified algorithm. \cref{sec:experiments} compares it with the original and with four other methods in five simulation experiments, and examines the sensitivity of the results to the bandwidth. \cref{sec:applications} applies both versions to two real datasets, and \cref{sec:conclusion} discusses the results.
