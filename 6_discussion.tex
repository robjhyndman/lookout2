\section{Discussion and conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

The bandwidth of the original lookout algorithm was defined through the death diameters of a Vietoris-Rips filtration, which are the edge lengths of the Euclidean minimum spanning tree of the data. Working with the tree has allowed us to analyse the bandwidth and to modify the algorithm in three ways. The data are standardized with the MCD estimator, the bandwidth is set using an upper quantile of the edge lengths in place of the edge length at the lower end of the largest gap, and the shape parameter of the generalized Pareto distribution is constrained to be non-positive.

For any density, $n^{1/m}h_n$ converges in probability to a constant, so the quantile bandwidth $h_n$ has the order of the spacing between neighbouring observations. The bandwidth of the original algorithm uses the edge length at the lower end of the largest gap, which typically lies among the longest edges of the tree. These edges grow without bound for the heavy-tailed density of \cref{cex:fattails}, and for Gaussian data the longest edge tends to zero only as $\log\log n/\sqrt{\log n}$. Since $nh_n^m$ converges to a constant, the kernel density estimate with bandwidth $h_n$ is not MISE-consistent, and $h_n$ is asymptotically smaller than the AMISE-optimal bandwidth. In three or more dimensions and at practical sample sizes, $h_n$ is larger than the AMISE-optimal bandwidth, because $h_n$ is set by the spacing in the tails, where anomalies are identified, and the AMISE-optimal bandwidth by the bulk of the data. In \cref{sec:bwablation}, multiplying $h_n$ by factors from $1/2$ to $4$ changed the AUC little, and at $h_n$ the false positive rate was close to the nominal $\alpha$. Replacing $h_n$ by the AMISE-optimal bandwidth in six dimensions left more than 10\% of the observations isolated in most runs, and the generalized Pareto distribution could not be fitted.

In the simulations the new version has a higher true positive rate than the original in Experiment 1, higher Fmeasure and Gmean in Experiments 3 and 4 once the anomalies begin to move away from the other observations, and a higher AUC in the later iterations of Experiment 5. The two versions perform similarly in Experiment 2 and in Experiment S1 of the online Appendix, and the original is slightly better at the largest sample sizes of Experiment S2. The new version is ahead of HDoutliers, stray, KDEOS and RDOS on every metric in Experiment 4 and from the fourth iteration of Experiment 3. RDOS has a higher AUC in the first three iterations of Experiment 3 and in most iterations up to the eleventh of Experiment 5, after which the two are similar. HDoutliers and stray never identify the anomaly in Experiment 5. In the Old Faithful and wine reviews data the new version flags observations in sparse regions, and the original also flags observations at the edge of the data that are close to other observations.

Two choices affect which observations are identified. The first is the quantile level. With $\gamma=0.98$, about 2\% of the observations are still isolated when the single linkage dendrogram is cut at height $h_n$. If more than about $1-\gamma$ of the observations are isolated anomalies, $h_n$ is set by the spacing between them, so a smaller $\gamma$ should be used when a large proportion of anomalies is expected. The second is the scaling. The new version standardizes the data with the full MCD covariance matrix, so the tree and the kernel density estimate depend on the data only through robust Mahalanobis distances between observations. Since the MCD estimator is affine equivariant, the observations identified are unchanged by any invertible affine transformation of the data. Min--max scaling is unaffected only by shifting and rescaling each variable separately, and it accounts for most of the difference between the two versions in the wine reviews data. The MCD estimate requires more observations than variables, and is unstable when $n$ is less than about $2m$. When its covariance matrix is singular, the implementation adds a small ridge to it. The scaling can also be turned off, as in Experiment 5, where all variables have the same scale.

The algorithm applies to any continuous multivariate data, and in the experiments it performs well in up to 20 dimensions. It can be applied to problems that can be transformed to that setting, such as image anomaly detection through feature extraction or functional data through functional principal components. It assumes independent and identically distributed observations, so temporal or spatial data need pre-processing, for example by applying the algorithm to the residuals of a fitted model. In high dimensions kernel density estimation suffers from the curse of dimensionality, and dimension reduction such as principal components or dobin \citep{dobin} before applying lookout may help.

The tree is computed by the dual-tree Bor\r{u}vka algorithm of \citet{March2010} in $O(n\log n)$ time under the same assumptions that make dual-tree nearest-neighbour search efficient. It takes a few seconds for $n=10^6$ in two dimensions and about a minute for $n=10^5$ in ten (\cref{tab:timing}). The kernel density estimate dominates the cost. The exact estimate uses a fixed-radius search, whose cost is proportional to the number of pairs of observations inside the kernel support. In three or more dimensions the support in the bulk of the data holds a large fraction of the sample, so the exact estimate is practical only for $n$ up to about $10^4$. For larger $n$ the implementation uses a truncated sum over nearest neighbours, which takes two and a half minutes for $n=10^5$ in ten dimensions.

Two theoretical questions remain open. We have no formula for where the largest gap between consecutive edge lengths occurs, so the bandwidth of the original algorithm has not been analysed beyond the results on the longest edge. The constant $q_\gamma(f)$ of \cref{prop:order} is defined through the component count of a Poisson process and is not explicit, although its bounds $t_-$ and $t_+$ are.
