\section{Discussion and conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Working with the minimum spanning tree in place of the Rips filtration has allowed us to analyse the bandwidth of the lookout algorithm. The quantile bandwidth $h_n$ has the order $n^{-1/m}$ of the spacing between neighbouring observations for any density, while the largest-gap bandwidth of the original algorithm can grow without bound under heavy tails. The resulting density estimate is not MISE-consistent. In \cref{sec:bwablation} the ranking of the observations changed little when $h_n$ was multiplied by factors from $1/2$ to $4$. In the simulations the new version performs better than the original or about as well, except at the largest sample sizes of Experiment S2. It is ahead of the four other methods, except that RDOS has a higher AUC in the early iterations of Experiments 3 and 5.

Two choices affect which observations are identified. The first is the quantile level. By the single linkage interpretation of \eqref{eq:mstbw}, at most a proportion of about $1-\gamma$ of the observations are isolated at $h_n$. If a larger proportion are isolated anomalies, $h_n$ is set by the spacing between them, so a smaller $\gamma$ should be used when a large proportion of anomalies is expected. The second is the scaling. The new version standardizes the data with the full MCD covariance matrix, so the tree and the kernel density estimate depend on the data only through robust Mahalanobis distances between observations. Since the MCD estimator is affine equivariant, the observations identified are unchanged by any invertible affine transformation of the data. Min--max scaling is unaffected only by shifting and rescaling each variable separately, and it accounts for most of the difference between the two versions in the wine reviews data. The MCD estimate requires more observations than variables, and is unstable when $n$ is less than about $2m$. When its covariance matrix is singular, the implementation adds a small ridge to it. The scaling can also be turned off, as in Experiment 5, where all variables have the same scale.

The algorithm applies to any continuous multivariate data, and in the experiments it performs well in up to 20 dimensions. It can be applied to problems that can be transformed to that setting, such as image anomaly detection through feature extraction or functional data through functional principal components. It assumes independent and identically distributed observations, so temporal or spatial data need pre-processing, for example by applying the algorithm to the residuals of a fitted model. In high dimensions kernel density estimation suffers from the curse of dimensionality, and dimension reduction such as principal components or dobin \citep{dobin} before applying lookout may help.

The tree is computed in $O(n\log n)$ time \citep{March2010}, and the cost is dominated by the kernel density estimate, for which the implementation uses a truncated sum over nearest neighbours when $n>10^4$ (\cref{sec:modified}).

Two theoretical questions remain open. We have no formula for where the largest gap between consecutive edge lengths occurs, so the bandwidth of the original algorithm has not been analysed beyond the results on the longest edge. The constant $q_\gamma(f)$ of \cref{prop:order} is defined through the component count of a Poisson process and is not explicit, although its bounds $t_-$ and $t_+$ are.
